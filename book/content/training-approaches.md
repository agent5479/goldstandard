# Where Gold Standard sits among training approaches

Dog training is a crowded and often heated field. Two trainers can use the same words, such as "positive", "balanced", "leadership", or "correction", and mean very different things. This chapter places Gold Standard on the map. It sets out what the method does and does not use, how it compares with Beckman-style training (the influence Warwick acknowledges) and with the other main schools, and what the research, professional bodies, and New Zealand's welfare framework say. The aim is clarity rather than persuasion: you should be able to see exactly what kind of method this is before you commit your dog to it.

## A shared vocabulary: how dogs learn

Most modern training discussion uses the language of learning theory. In that language, *reinforcement* makes a behaviour more likely and *punishment* makes it less likely. *Positive* means something is added and *negative* means something is taken away. None of these words is a moral judgement: "positive punishment" simply means adding something the dog wants to avoid, whether that is a sharp word or a leash pop.

| Quadrant | What happens | Everyday example | Where it appears in Gold Standard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Positive reinforcement** | Add something the dog wants | Food, praise, a game, access to a sniff | [Access as the reward](/guide/training#access); calm praise after a time buffer; food used selectively as [handler reinforcement](/guide/training#rewards) |
| **Negative reinforcement** | Remove pressure when the dog complies | Leash tension released the moment the dog steps back into position | Pressure-and-release on a flat collar or slip lead; the release is the feedback |
| **Positive punishment** | Add something unpleasant to stop a behaviour | Leash pop, sharp "Hey!", a push, a pinch collar, a shock | The [verbal pop](/guide/training#verbal-correction), [butt push](/guide/training#butt-push), [downward leash jerk](/guide/training#leash-jerk), [collar grab](/guide/training#collar-snatch), brief squeeze, and [pin & hold](/guide/training#pin-hold) |
| **Negative punishment** | Remove something the dog wants | Walking away from a jumping dog, ending play, clipping the leash back on | Access removed when the standard slips; the leash goes back on |

Two further ideas matter. *Classical conditioning* is learning by association: a sound paired again and again with a physical correction becomes a correction in its own right, which is how Gold Standard's verbal pop eventually replaces the hand. *Antecedent management* means arranging the environment so that the problem behaviour is less likely in the first place: leads, muzzles, barriers, routines, and exercise. Every serious method uses all of these to some degree. What separates methods is the *balance* between them, *which* aversives (if any) are acceptable, *how* they are delivered, and the *explanation* the method gives for why dogs behave as they do.

## The main approaches today

The table below is a simplification. Many trainers blend approaches, and each label covers a wide range of practice.

| Approach | Core idea | Typical tools and techniques | Aversives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Force-free / positive reinforcement only** | Build wanted behaviour with rewards; manage and redirect the rest; change emotional responses with desensitisation and counter-conditioning | Markers or clickers, food, play, management, harnesses | Avoided entirely (beyond mild negative punishment such as ending a game) |
| **LIMA and the Humane Hierarchy** | Use the "least intrusive, minimally aversive" option that works, in a fixed order: health, environment, positive reinforcement, differential reinforcement, then negative punishment or extinction, and positive punishment only as a last resort | As above, with a formal decision process | Permitted in principle as a last resort; many practitioners avoid positive punishment altogether |
| **Reinforcement-led, correction-inclusive** (e.g. Beckman's Dog Training) | Teach with rewards, fade them quickly into life rewards, and enforce known behaviour with brief corrections | Food and praise for new behaviours, "nothing for free" routines, leash pops on a flat collar or head halter, calm helper dogs | Yes, mild to moderate, delivered through the leash |
| **Balanced training with devices** | Combine rewards with corrections delivered through a tool | Prong collars, remote electronic collars, slip leads | Yes; the device often delivers most of the correction |
| **Traditional compulsion** (e.g. the Koehler method) | The dog is shown what to do, then compelled to do it; errors are corrected firmly | Choke chains, long lines, strong leash corrections; little or no food | Yes, often strong |
| **Dominance or "energy" based** (e.g. Cesar Millan's televised approach) | Problem behaviour reflects a lack of calm, assertive pack leadership; the handler claims leadership | Calm-assertive handler state, touch corrections, body blocks, rank rules, pack walks, helper dogs | Yes, physical; historically associated with pinning ("alpha rolls") |
| **Constructional and relationship approaches** (e.g. Behaviour Adjustment Training) | Give reactive dogs controlled choice and distance so they learn calm alternatives | Long lines, carefully placed decoy dogs at a distance below the reaction threshold | Avoided |

## Where Gold Standard sits

Gold Standard is a **balanced, handler-delivered, relationship-based** method. It is easiest to place on three separate dimensions rather than a single line.

**How much the method relies on aversives.** Gold Standard uses all four quadrants, and positive punishment is a routine and deliberate part of the toolkit rather than a last resort. The About chapter says so plainly: firm physical resets are "not optional add-ons, but how the boundary becomes real". Intensity runs up an [escalation ladder](/guide/training#escalation-ladder): verbal pop; butt push or downward leash jerk; collar grab and forced sit; a brief "shocking squeeze" with the hand acting as a mouth would; and, at the very top, a pin & hold that is taught only in sessions. Most days never leave the lower rungs, and the guide lists clear situations where firmer treatment is *not* appropriate: dogs showing [trauma signals](/guide/understanding#trauma-signals), young puppies, an angry or exhausted handler, and cases where lighter tools have not been tried consistently. On this dimension Gold Standard sits beyond Beckman-style training and closer to traditional and dominance-based methods, particularly at the top of the ladder.

**How aversives are delivered.** Here Gold Standard sits at the opposite end from device-based balanced training. It excludes prong collars, shock, vibration, and remote collars entirely, and it avoids harsh choke-chain corrections. The handler's voice, hand, and a flat collar or slip lead are the only means of correction, because the method holds that authority must stay in the relationship where it can be timed, calibrated, and released. A head halter is a coached exception for handler safety, not a teaching tool. Chest and front-clip harnesses are excluded because the method holds that they encourage pulling. On this dimension Gold Standard sits closer to force-free trainers than to most balanced trainers, although for different reasons.

**How the method explains behaviour.** Gold Standard's explanatory model is social and ethological rather than purely mechanical. It speaks of pack structure, rank, native canine language, and the handler's embodied state (**somatic regulation** and **autonomic co-regulation** — which Warwick sometimes calls [Dog-Tantra](/guide/leadership#dog-tantra) in sessions, meaning physiological alignment and pack sensitivity rather than anything mystical). Learning-theory ideas such as the one-second timing window, pressure and release, and conditioned verbal markers sit inside that social frame. This places Gold Standard alongside leadership-based methods rather than the learning-theory methods of force-free trainers and, to a large extent, Beckman's.

In short, Gold Standard is **device-free but not force-free**. It is more hands-on and more correction-reliant than Beckman-style training, and more relationship- and embodiment-focused than traditional compulsion.

## Gold Standard and Beckman's Dog Training

Beckman's Dog Training was founded by Joel and Liz Beckman, former SeaWorld marine-mammal trainers who now train dogs in San Diego County, California. Through a very large online audience they have popularised a style of training that many people now call "Beckman-style". In their public materials they describe themselves as a positive-reinforcement-based programme: new behaviours are taught with food and praise, food is faded quickly in favour of "nothing for free" routines and natural life rewards, and known behaviours are enforced with brief leash corrections on a flat collar or a Gentle Leader head halter. They say publicly that they do not use shock or prong collars. They also use calm, well-socialised dogs to help rehabilitate reactive ones. Critics from the force-free community argue that some of their reactive-dog work amounts to flooding and that their corrections are aversive; supporters point to calm, reliable dogs and to their refusal to use devices.

The About chapter describes Beckman-style training as the "broader structure-and-owner-coaching philosophy that influenced Warwick's approach", and is explicit that this is not a credential, an affiliation, or an endorsement. The comparison below is based on Beckman's public materials, not on any inside knowledge of their programme.

| Topic | Beckman's Dog Training (public materials) | Gold Standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **How the method describes itself** | Positive-reinforcement based | Structure- and relationship-based; openly includes firm physical correction |
| **Teaching new behaviour** | Food and praise, faded quickly into life rewards | Repetition and clarity; access and freedom as the main reward; food used selectively; praise held back until a time buffer after any correction |
| **Enforcing known behaviour** | Brief leash corrections | Unique sound, then unique touch: verbal pop, butt push, downward leash jerk, collar grab |
| **Upper limit of correction** | Leash corrections on a flat collar or head halter | A brief "shocking squeeze", and pin & hold demonstrated in sessions only |
| **Devices** | No shock or prong collars; head halters used | No shock, prong, vibration, or remote collars; head halter as a coached safety exception; chest and front-clip harnesses excluded |
| **Explanatory model** | Learning theory, drawn from professional animal-training practice | Pack language, rank, and the handler's embodied state, with learning-theory timing inside that frame |
| **Dog-to-dog work** | Calm helper dogs to model and support reactive dogs | Controlled Confrontation: a balanced helper dog delivers physical social correction under slack safety lines (with muzzles when needed) — indicated only for confident, socially pushy dogs, not fear-reactive or shutdown dogs |

**Common ground.** Both methods coach the owner at least as much as the dog. Both expect a dog to earn freedom through reliable behaviour, both refuse shock and prong collars, both treat leash timing as a core handler skill, and both see a well-behaved adult dog as a partner in rehabilitation.

**The important differences.** Gold Standard is not simply Beckman's method delivered in Golden Bay. It relies less on food, frames behaviour in terms of pack structure rather than reinforcement schedules, and goes further in two respects. First, the upper rungs of its escalation ladder, the shocking squeeze and the pin & hold, go beyond the leash corrections Beckman's are publicly known for. Second, in Controlled Confrontation the helper dog is used to *deliver* physical correction, not only to model calm behaviour. These elements are Gold Standard's own and should not be attributed to Beckman's. The same applies to Gold Standard's framework on desexing intact males, which the guide itself notes is controversial in veterinary circles.

## Gold Standard and energy- or dominance-based training

The About chapter distinguishes Gold Standard from what it calls "energy dominance" (Millan-style): projecting authority outward to *demand* submission through willpower. Gold Standard describes its own aim as embodied alignment, holding a baseline so clear that the dog relaxes into it. The guide repeatedly insists that correction must come from a calm handler, be calibrated to the individual dog, and be released the instant the message lands, never delivered in anger or "harder until they break".

Readers comparing methods should nonetheless know that, seen from outside, Gold Standard shares several features with dominance-based approaches: a calm-assertive handler state, pack and rank vocabulary, touch corrections, pinning a dog until it surrenders, and helper dogs that correct other dogs. The difference Gold Standard claims lies in intent, sensitivity, and calibration rather than in the techniques themselves. That is a meaningful distinction for the handler, but it does not change how the techniques are classified in the research literature. The criticisms of dominance-based training set out below therefore apply to those parts of this method.

## Gold Standard and force-free or LIMA training

There is more common ground here than the heat of the wider debate suggests. Gold Standard also asks owners to rule out medical causes, to read body language and early signs of stress, to [substitute rather than suppress](/guide/understanding#substitute-not-suppress) behaviours driven by unmet needs, to use management (leads, muzzles, controlled meetings) until a dog is ready, to lower intensity for dogs with trauma histories, and never to correct from frustration.

The differences are fundamental, however. In force-free training, positive punishment is excluded. In LIMA practice it is a documented last resort, used only after health, environment, and reinforcement-based options have been tried. In Gold Standard it is an everyday tool, and the method deliberately avoids heavy use of food. Force-free and LIMA trainers generally treat fear and reactivity as emotional states to be changed through gradual, below-threshold exposure. Gold Standard treats confident pushiness as a boundary problem that may use firm physical resets or Controlled Confrontation, while explicitly [contraindicating helper-dog confrontation](/guide/social#controlled-confrontation-contraindications) for fear-reactive, trauma-overflow, or shutdown-prone dogs in favour of sub-threshold distance work (such as BAT-aligned setups). Still, on the everyday toolkit for pushy or entitled dogs, the About chapter puts it directly: "If firm physical boundaries feel wrong for how you want to handle your dog, this method is not the right fit." Owners who want a force-free or LIMA approach should look for a trainer who works that way.

## What the research says

Research on training methods has grown quickly over the past twenty years. It is worth summarising honestly, including its limits.

**Welfare and stress.** Studies comparing reward-based and aversive-based training consistently associate aversive methods with more signs of stress. In a 2020 study of 92 companion dogs in Portugal, Vieira de Castro and colleagues found that dogs from schools using mainly aversive methods showed more stress behaviours during training and larger rises in cortisol, and later made more "pessimistic" judgements in a cognitive bias test. Dogs from schools using a mix of rewards and aversives fell in between on several measures. Deldalle and Gaunet (2014) found that dogs trained with leash-and-collar corrections showed more stress signals and looked at their owners less than dogs trained with rewards. Hiby, Rooney and Bradshaw (2004), in an owner survey, linked reward-based training with better obedience and punishment with more reported problem behaviours.

**Confrontational techniques and aggression.** The most directly relevant study for this book is Herron, Shofer and Reisner (2009). Owners attending a veterinary behaviour clinic reported how their dogs had responded to techniques they had previously tried. Several confrontational techniques drew an aggressive response from at least a quarter of the dogs they were used on, including the "alpha roll" (31%), the "dominance down" (29%), and grabbing the dog by the jowls and shaking (26%). Dogs presented for aggression towards familiar people were more likely to respond aggressively to the alpha roll. Techniques of this kind correspond most closely to the top of Gold Standard's escalation ladder. That is why the guide restricts them to guided sessions, forbids home improvisation, and contrasts emotional "alpha rolls" against its [Law of Conservation of Force](/guide/training#herron-study-contrast) — requiring zero anger, contextual receptivity, instant release, immediate redirection, and a neutral baseline with no correction-and-praise loop.

**Effectiveness.** A 2020 trial by China, Mills and Cooper found that reward-based training was more effective than remote electronic collar training for recall and sit problems, even when the electronic collars were used by experienced trainers. Reviews by Ziv (2017) and Fernandes and colleagues (2017) concluded that the evidence points to welfare risks from aversive methods and gives no sign that they are more effective than reward-based ones.

**The limits of the evidence.** Much of this research is observational or relies on owner surveys, so it shows associations rather than proving cause. "Aversive" is often a broad category that lumps together very different tools and intensities, and there is little research specifically on the kind of calibrated, instantly released, handler-delivered correction Gold Standard teaches. Fernandes and colleagues noted these methodological weaknesses and called for better studies. Still, a lack of research on a particular style of correction is not evidence that it is safe. Where the evidence is strongest, on confrontational physical techniques provoking defensive aggression, it applies directly to the most forceful parts of this method.

**The dominance debate.** Much of the "alpha" model of dog behaviour came from studies of unrelated captive wolves. Mech (1999) showed that wild wolf packs are usually families led by the parents, not groups held together by constant rank contests. Bradshaw, Blackwell and Casey (2009) argued that dominance is a poor model for the dog–human relationship and encourages confrontational training. Schilder, Vinke and van der Borg (2014), and later van der Borg and colleagues (2015), replied that dominance relationships between dogs are real and measurable. Even they, however, did not argue that people should establish rank over dogs through physical force. The current mainstream position is that dominance can be a useful way to describe some relationships *between dogs*, but it does not justify physically asserting rank over a pet dog. Readers should weigh the pack and rank language in this book with that debate in mind.

## What professional bodies and New Zealand's framework say

**Veterinary behaviour bodies.** The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) position statement on humane dog training (2021) recommends reward-based methods only. It states that aversive methods, including leash jerks, prong and choke collars, shock, alpha rolls, and physical intimidation, should not be used for training or for treating behaviour problems. AVSAB's earlier position statement on dominance theory (2008) advises against using dominance as the basis for behaviour modification. The New Zealand Veterinary Association does not support electronic training collars and recommends reward-based training. SPCA New Zealand opposes the production, sale, and use of pinch and prong collars.

**Training and behaviour organisations.** The International Association of Animal Behavior Consultants (IAABC) requires members to follow LIMA and opposes methods that rely on pain, fear, or intimidation. The Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers (CCPDT) and the Association of Professional Dog Trainers (APDT) endorse the Humane Hierarchy, under which positive punishment is a last resort.

**New Zealand law and welfare codes.** Measured leash, voice, and touch corrections are lawful in New Zealand. The legal framework sets clear limits, though:

- The **Animal Welfare Act 1999** places a duty of care on owners and makes ill-treatment an offence.
- The **Code of Welfare: Dogs (2018)**, Minimum Standard 19, requires that training aids "must not be used in a way that causes unreasonable or unnecessary pain or distress" and that "pinch or prong collars must not be used". Its recommended best practice says electronic devices should be used only when other methods have failed, by trained operators, and not on nervous dogs, and that choke chains should be correctly fitted and used only in supervised training sessions.
- On behaviour, the Code's recommended best practice says: "The owner's dominant position should be enforced calmly and patiently using recognised techniques that provide positive reinforcement for acceptable behaviour as part of the training process. Dominance should not be established by physical punishment or processes that are psychologically harmful to the dog."
- The **Animal Welfare (Care and Procedures) Regulations 2018** make it an infringement offence for a collar or tether to cause cuts, abrasions, or swelling, or to stop a dog breathing, panting, or drinking normally.
- The **Dog Control Act 1996** makes owners responsible for keeping their dog under control, and requires dogs classified as menacing or dangerous to be muzzled in public.
- Dog training is **not a regulated profession** in New Zealand. Anyone may call themselves a trainer or behaviourist, and membership of voluntary associations is optional.

**What this means for Gold Standard.** Gold Standard's exclusion of prong, shock, vibration, and remote collars sits comfortably inside New Zealand's framework and goes further than the law requires. Its everyday leash, voice, and touch corrections are lawful, but they fall outside what AVSAB and the NZVA recommend. The pin & hold in particular is the kind of technique that those bodies, and the Code's best-practice guidance on how dominance should (and should not) be established, advise against. The guide's position is that "force without calibration can traumatise; force *with* calibration can clarify", and that calibrated, instantly released, sessions-only use by a calm handler is different in kind from the confrontational punishment those statements describe. That is Warwick's professional judgement, and it is a minority view among veterinary behaviour organisations. You deserve to know both before you decide.

## Choosing with your eyes open

Whichever approach you are considering, from this trainer or any other, these questions help:

1. **What exactly will you do to my dog when it gets something wrong?** Ask for a demonstration or description of the firmest thing the trainer might do.
2. **What tools do you use, and which do you refuse?** And why?
3. **How do you adjust for fear, pain, or trauma?** A good answer includes a vet check and a lower-intensity plan.
4. **What will I be doing at home, and what is sessions-only?**
5. **What do you do if my dog gets worse?** Look for willingness to refer to a veterinarian or a veterinary behaviourist.

Gold Standard is a good fit for owners who want a structured, hands-on method with firm and clearly explained boundaries, no correction devices, and a strong emphasis on the handler's own calm and consistency. It is not a good fit for owners who want a force-free or LIMA-only approach, or for whom any physical correction feels wrong. If your dog shows significant fear, anxiety, or aggression, consider a veterinary behaviour consultation alongside any training. The guide itself calls for lower intensity and a trust-first approach in exactly those cases.
